The Velvet Knife is a dark, street-level fantasy heist series about a morally gray thief and the crew she gathers to steal the magical machines that let cities own people — one impossible job at a time, until the whole system is the mark.

Nasyk is a thief. Small, blue, very good at climbing things and taking things that don't belong to her. She works with crews she cannot quite afford to trust, in cities that would cheerfully see her dead, for money that is — for once — clean.

Every book is a heist inside a different city — each one with its own machinery of power, its own ugly door, its own reason you'd be a fool to try. Beneath them runs the Sixfold Claim: six houses, in six of the oldest cities, each owning a piece of what it means to be a person. Nasyk isn't selling her luck for gold. She's tearing down the machinery that owns people, one city at a time.

Street-level fantasy. No prophecy. No chosen one. Just a very small woman, a very ugly door, and the one crew that might — might — be good enough.

The Silver Vow — Book Two — Training

Old Marvo's Lesson

"Guard. And mind the gondola, I've a good window seat."

We go at it, and here's the thing that took me a year to understand about Marvo: he doesn't teach me the form. He teaches me how to survive it. The needle — Serel's needle, finally worn out in the open where I can reach it, on my right hip, ready for the crossdraw, no more shame in it than a blacksmith's arm — is a long thin thing for a long thin woman, and I'm four foot eleven and a half — the half matters, I've never once dropped it — and built like a boy who never got fed, and the forms were written for taller people with patience.

Marvo looked at me for a solid week when I first came to him and said, "You fight like you fell down the stairs. And it works. So I'm not going to change that."

Then he set about making the staircase mine.

So we don't do what the manuals do. We do what works. He'll come at me with the old style, perfect, unhurried, and I'll do something ugly and fast and gravel-under-the-foot, and half the time it works and half the time he's already caught me and I'm tasting the flagstone. And he'll stand over me breathing like a bellows and say, "You just countered a man who's been doing this since before your grandmother was a splinter, with a move you learned from a sewer drain. I want you to understand that's not a compliment." And then, because he's a sentimental bastard under the fist-face, he'll help me up and say, "Again."

After, he sat me on the bench and stood looking at the canal with his hands behind his back, which is how he stands when he's deciding whether to say something. "You're carrying something," he said, to the canal.

"I'm fine."

"I didn't ask if you were fine. I said you're carrying something. The body doesn't lie in a drill. Whatever's in your head is in your hands by the second measure, and your hands have been half a beat behind all morning." He turned. "You don't have to tell me what it is. But I need you to know I can see it, so you're not managing both the problem and the impression of no problem. The impression costs as much as the thing."

"Personal stakes change the body's arithmetic," he said. "It starts calculating not just the risk to itself but the risk of failing what it cares about. That's two calculations at once." He picked up his flask. "Compensating makes it worse. Knowing it's there and letting it be there — that's different from compensating. You don't fight the lag. You don't pretend it's not there. You simply know where it is and work around it, the way you work around a sore ankle without loading it. That's a thing some people learn in thirty years of fighting. You learned it now because you have the sense to tell someone."

The House That Keeps the Names — Book Three — The Burial

The Ground

I made my lungs go slow when every cell of me screamed to gulp. Slow. In through the nose, the cloth pulling to my lips, a count, out. A thief's whole life is stealing time; I lay in my own grave and stole breaths off my own panic, one at a time, and it is the only theft I've ever been ashamed to be good at.

I could move — a hand's width, no more, in any direction. I could turn my head until my nose fouled the wood. I could lift a knee until it met the lid. I could spread my fingers and touch, left and right, the walls of the whole world, and the world was the width of my own shoulders and no wider. You do not know what your body is for until it is given nothing to do and nowhere to go. Mine had spent its life as a spare quick length made for walls and gaps and the tops of things, and now it was folded into a box the shape of a person, and every muscle in it woke up wanting to run and found the running had been taken away, all at once, forever.

There's no clock in the ground. Three days my candle would burn, my little flame, whether I lay to watch it or not, and when it guttered the wax would seal and the death would set and everything I had would fall to the house, and I would be, at last and in law, exactly what they'd written.

But: the dead keep their steel.

It is the one dignity that grim city gave its corpses. They hadn't known to look for it, or hadn't cared to, or the quiet man who dressed me for the hole had felt the hard length at the small of my back and left it because the dead keep their steel and it was more than his soul was worth to send a corpse down disarmed. And there, bound in old leather where it had ridden since I was young enough to steal it, was the Needle.

I set the point to the lid above my face and I began to work it into the seam. I'll not pretend it was quick, or that I was brave through it. I wept, in the box, the first real weeping I'd done in decades, snot and dark and the ants pouring and the air going bad, and I dug through it, because you can weep and dig at the same time — that's a thing the ground taught me, that despair and work aren't the enemies the churches say they are. You can be broken all the way through and your hands can keep moving, and the hands are what get you out. Not the courage. The hands.

My hand broke the surface into nothing, into cold, into wet. I came up out of the earth of that city the way I've come into every city in my life — sideways, uninvited, hunting the nearest air. The rain came down and washed the dirt and blood off my body, and I got a whole breath, the first whole breath, and I laughed. Wet, and cracked, and mad, half in and half out of a hole in a paupers' field in the rain.

They thought me in the ground. And here was the joke the house had made without seeing it: my money wasn't theirs. Not yet.

The Silver Vow — Book Two — First Night in Vael Corran

On the Roof

I marked my roads while I had the height. This is the discipline: you learn three ways out before a single way in, because in is want and out is life, and want will get you killed if you let it lead. The roofs east toward the docks — good broken ground, plenty of gaps a leaper could take. The aqueduct coming in from the marsh hills on its long arches, and the dark beneath it where a body could live if it had to. Where the drains must be, by the slope of the streets. Water and thieves go the same road.

Then, with the copper warm under my cheek and the day gone quiet, I did the thing I always do when there’s food in me and no one to see. I loosened the thong on the bundle. I never fully unwrap it. I let a hand’s width of blade show — narrow, pale, the alloy the colour of a winter dawn, a Nethershade needle that seemed to drink light rather than throw it — and felt the whole ugly knot of it turn over in my chest. I laced it back up before the feeling could set a hook. That much I’d not give even the empty roof.

The day cooled off my skin as the sun went, and I opened the suede to let it. I’m built spare as a lath, flat as a boy across the chest and flatter behind — nothing to grab there either, which has earned me cruelty in some quarters and got me over walls in all of them, and pleased the right hands besides. When the first of the moon came up over the copper I watched, idle, the way the cold drew my nipples up long and dark against the pale blue of me, two small stubborn points of shadow, and I ran a thumb over one just to feel it stand, unhurried, the only ornament I’ll ever own that no House can put a sum to. I’m not vain. I’ve simply spent enough of my life being looked at with a sign drawn against me that I take a private pleasure, now and then, in looking at myself with none, and liking what I find. Then I pulled the suede closed, because the roof was cold and sentiment is cheaper than wine and twice as quick to make a fool of you.

So there I was, on a warm roof in a strange city, the money-bells tolling, the bad-toothed grin of Vael Corran laid out below — fed for the first time in three days and near to happy, in the low animal way I get happy: hidden, warm, and not yet anybody’s.

That last part was going to be the trouble.

The Drowned Masque — Book Four — After

Still Here

I poured a cup of warm spiced wine. Set it on the table beside the blade.

"Still here," I said, to the cup, to the needle, to the pale-going dark outside the window. "Still yours."

I picked up the needle. Wiped it properly. Held it in the lamp-light the way Serel had held it in the dark, in a room I'd been certain I'd die in, and watched the light move in the steel. The velvet knife. The name they'd breathed into me at the end of one life and the beginning of the next.

Below, the canal was beginning to catch the first of the morning, the water going from black to grey to the particular thin gold that means the city is deciding to be a city again. On the bridge, two canal birds were having an argument, unimpressed by the night's events, as they are — and suddenly made me think of Kell, somewhere far off with a stash of bright loot in a salt marsh, fledglings to call his own.

Somewhere in the city, the kept were filing home without their masques. The register was empty. The mechanism that had been running for four centuries had been opened and the water was gone and the forgetting could drain the way forgetting was supposed to drain — naturally, without weight, into the sea.

It's not a clean ending. Once again. Opening a pool doesn't fix a city — it removes the machine that was running it, and what fills the gap is the city's own problem, and cities have a long history of filling gaps badly.

The kept are still rich and the maskless are still poor and the upper canal is still the upper canal. I didn't fix that. I broke a machine.

But the pool is open. The water is gone. And four centuries of accumulated forgetting, all the dammed weight of names the city had been made to unknow, is draining the way the natural tide drains.

And on the landing above mine, a widow who has been paying for a fiction is free of the expense. As for the Merchant, he is in his wagon on a canal I won't find again. The Sea Witch and the Scroll Keeper are somewhere in the tide and reflections and the ink, and they have a thief who does what she is asked to do, and next time they will ask her to do it somewhere else, and she will go.
